## Tennis Background — What You Need to Know

This project analyzes personal match data using Swing Vision, a tennis tracking app. If you are not familiar with tennis, this section covers the key concepts and terminology that will help you understand the analysis.

---

### How Tennis Is Scored

A general rule of thumb is the useful framework for thinking about tennis strategy with the **51% rule**: you do not need to win every point, you just need to win slightly more than you lose... i.e. 51% of the points. Of course, this is not a rule that guarantees a win, however, its a high indicator and when broken down like this, tennis is much more digestable.  If a strategy wins you the point 51% of the time, keep doing it. If it only works 49% of the time, you shouldn't continue. This sounds simple but it has a big impact on decision-making during a match, meaning every shot choice should be evaluated not on whether it worked once, but on whether it works consistently over time.

### Court Zones and Shot Directions

Understanding where shots land is central to this project. Here are the key terms:

**Deep** — a shot that lands close to the opponent's baseline, giving them less time to react and pushing them back behind the court.

**Short** — a shot that lands closer to the net, inside the service boxes. Short balls can be attackable opportunities or, when keep very low) can be tactical weapons depending on the situation.

**Cross court** — a shot hit diagonally across the court, from one corner to the opposite corner. This is the longest and generally safest available angle and the most common direction in rallies.

**Down the line** — a shot hit straight along the sideline. This is a shorter distance and a riskier target, but it changes the angle of the rally and can catch opponents off guard.

**Wide** — on a serve, a ball aimed at the outer edge of the service box toward the sideline, pulling the opponent off the court.

**Down the T** — on a serve, a ball aimed at the center line of the service box. This is called "the T" because of the shape the center and service lines form.

**Volley** — a type of stroke hit before the ball bounces, typically when a player has moved up to the net.

**Drop shot** — a type of stroke hit shot designed to land just over the net and bounce very short, forcing the opponent to sprint forward.

**Slice** — a shot stroke with backspin, causing the ball to stay low after the bounce.

### How I Play

My game is built around variety and dictating points rather than winning through power alone. A few things to know about my style that make this analysis more meaningful:

I hit **slice on every backhand** meaning I do not hit topspin backhands at all. Keep this in mind as swing vision catagorizes all shots on my left side a backhand and adds an addition spin catagory as slice thereby not truly identifing a "backhand slice" alone. This is an uncommon choice at the competitive level and it shapes how I construct points. The slice keeps the ball low and forces opponents to hit up, which can open the court for my forehand.

My general strategy is to **control points with my forehand** and use that to set up a finish: either coming to the net for a volley, drawing the opponent short with a drop shot, or forcing an error by mixing in slice at unexpected moments. The variety is intentional: changing pace, spin, and direction keeps opponents from settling into a rhythm.

This style means the most important sections of this analysis are not just win/loss numbers, but the *tactical context* . Understanding this justifies my general theme of wanting to analyze what shots lead to good outcomes, when drop shots and slices work, and whether the variety is actually translating into points won.
